# Understand the project before putting it on a resume

## The short explanation

The project asks whether a plan that looks cheap at a standard comparison point stays cheap when electricity use changes. It rebuilds the bill from documented pricing rules instead of treating an advertised average as a constant rate. One credit threshold makes a 999-kWh month cost more than a 1,000-kWh month. A second illustration holds total consumption fixed and changes how it is distributed across billing cycles.

## Be ready to explain

- Why a bill can fall when consumption rises: a fixed credit activates at a threshold.
- Why the $750 result is an identity under constructed profiles, not a measured effect for real consumers.
- Why older delivery charges cannot be mixed with new ones without labeling the basis.
- Why a nine-month contract cannot be ranked on a twelve-month cost without renewal assumptions.
- Why passing the three disclosed price checks does not prove every fee is covered.
- Why seven selected contracts do not establish the cheapest supplier in Houston.
- Why two different-duration plans can have identical current bills but different switching risks.
- Why there is no machine learning: the relevant price functions are specified by contract terms.

## A good extension

Obtain a consented consumption history and compare contracts for that specific history, clearly labeled as an individual illustration. A representative market study requires a systematic sampling frame and synchronized snapshots.

## Accurate possible resume bullet

Reconstructed seven Houston-area electricity contracts in Python and SQL, reconciled 21 published prices and built an interactive calculator examining usage-credit thresholds and equal-consumption scenarios.

Only use this after reviewing the sources, understanding the methods and verifying the delivered work yourself. Do not claim client work, actual household savings or a causal finding.
