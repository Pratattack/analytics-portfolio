# Same electricity, different bill

**Decision:** How should a Houston household compare a threshold-credit contract with a contract without a usage credit?

**Audience:** Consumers comparing pricing rules; pricing, energy and consumer-research analysts evaluating how disclosure points relate to actual billing formulas.

## Findings

1. In Discount Power Bill Credit Bundle 12, the modeled recurring bill is $197.47 at 999 kWh and $72.66 at 1,000 kWh. A $125 credit begins at the threshold. Reducing usage by 1 kWh therefore raises the modeled bill by $124.81. Source: label dated 4 October 2026, page 1; delivery charges from the provider table dated 4 October 2026.
2. The same plan yields a $750 cost difference between two hypothetical profiles totaling 12,000 kWh each. Flat use at 1,000 kWh in all twelve cycles earns $1,500 in credits. The uneven profile earns $750. With all other components held constant, the credit difference explains the entire bill difference.
3. A low advertised rate is not sufficient to rank a contract over a usage range. Against Discount Basic 36 Wind, the Bundle 12 modeled charge is higher below 1,000 kWh, lower from 1,000 to approximately 3,871 kWh, and higher above that crossing. The comparison isolates price structure; the contracts have different durations and exit fees.
4. All 21 disclosed comparison prices in the seven-plan sample reconcile within one-decimal-cent rounding. The largest absolute residual is about 0.0467 cents/kWh. This validates the modeled recurring components at the disclosure points; it is not a test of actual customer invoices or every possible fee.

## Decision implication

Use billing-cycle consumption, not only an annual average, to evaluate eligibility for credits. Examine how a contract performs just below and above every threshold. Compare contracts over a horizon they actually cover, and assess termination charges, prepayment and eligibility separately. A model should explain the choice before ranking it.

## What the evidence does not establish

The seven contracts are a purposive sample from four providers. Three labels predate October. Two contracts have identical recurring price functions. No claim of market coverage, cheapest available supplier, customer harm, price discrimination or realized savings is supported. Household profiles are hypothetical. Enrollment availability and address-specific eligibility have not been verified.

October normalization holds delivery charges at $4.90 per cycle and 6.5333 cents/kWh. That makes the pricing-rule comparison interpretable, but it is not a forecast of future utility tariffs. Twelve cycles of 30 days are modeled, not a 365-day calendar year. The Payless label reserves price-change discretion and any multi-cycle comparison assumes unchanged charges.

## Next evidence worth obtaining

A consented twelve-month household usage record would allow an individual illustration, while still avoiding population claims. An interval dataset is needed before comparing free-hours plans. A fuller market study would require systematic sampling, synchronized collection, eligibility checks, more provider coverage and repeated snapshots.
