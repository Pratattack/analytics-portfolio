# Crocs: interview notes

## Thirty-second explanation
The base scenario funds $600m of first-period repurchases and $134m of residual debt repayment. The case depends on assumed brand contribution margins. A lower share count alone does not establish an operating recovery. The model connects operating drivers to cash and financing, then asks what the dated market price requires. All future numbers are scenarios.

## Three findings
Buybacks and debt reduction compete for the same cash. First-period operating cash of $801m less $80m capex leaves $721m. Opening cash above the $150m reserve adds about $13m. After $600m repurchases, $134m remains for debt repayment.

A noncash charge is different from trading pressure. The downside case combines weaker sales and contribution margins with a hypothetical $200m nondeductible impairment. The charge reduces earnings and assets, but does not itself reduce cash. Downside operating cash is $363m and net debt / EBITDA reaches 1.73x. The impairment is not a forecast of an actual charge.

The base DCF has little room for error. Its $159.71 per-share indication is close to the dated $150.52 quote. With five cash flows fixed, the quote implies a 21.2% terminal EBIT margin at 11% WACC, 2% growth and 15% terminal ROIC. Terminal value contributes 64.4% of enterprise value.

## Most consequential choice
The most consequential operating choice is the contribution margin assigned to each brand before D&A and SG&A. Base Crocs contribution is 65%, with HEYDUDE at 46% rising to 48%. These are analyst assumptions, not disclosed brand cash margins. Because brand D&A allocation is unavailable, the model uses a combined PPE and finite-intangible depreciation pool.

## Best objection
The strongest argument against the base conclusion is that assumed contribution margins and modest SG&A improvement overstate sustainable cash profitability. The DCF range spans about $71 to $213 across the three operating cases at the same WACC and growth. This is a scenario range without assigned probabilities, not a statistical confidence interval.

## Questions to rehearse
1. Why do stronger profits not necessarily increase repurchase capacity?
2. Why does a noncash impairment affect assets and equity without an equal cash outflow?
3. Why use dated cover-page shares for market capitalization instead of weighted-average EPS shares?
4. What happens when refinancing is unavailable? Does the model assume a facility?
5. Why retain stock compensation as an economic cost in DCF?
6. What fraction of value comes after the explicit forecast, and what changes it?
7. Which cost and working-capital assumptions could reverse the conclusion?

## Explain the funding waterfall
Opening cash + CFO + CFI - principal repayment + assumed refinancing. Compare with the cash reserve. Show any additional funding required explicitly. Cap dividends and buybacks to available cash. Sweep residual cash to debt. Link closing cash, debt and equity back to the balance sheet. Interest uses opening debt to avoid circularity.

## Honest closing
Brand and channel contribution margins, promotion intensity, returns, unit volumes and prices, geographic tariff exposure and financing terms would most improve the analysis. Sell-in revenue is not proof of consumer sell-through. I would improve those inputs before presenting this as an investment recommendation.
