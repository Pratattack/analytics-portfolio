# United Rentals: interview notes

## Thirty-second explanation
The base scenario supports $597m of first-period repurchases against an illustrative $1,500m ceiling. Equipment investment and working capital absorb cash even as modeled revenue grows. The model connects operating drivers to cash and financing, then asks what the dated market price requires. All future numbers are scenarios.

## Three findings
Investment determines the distribution budget. Operating cash of $5,430m less net investing outflow of $4,336m leaves $1,094m. With opening cash of $153m, a $150m reserve and a $500m dividend budget, repurchases fall to $597m.

Stronger demand can still reduce buybacks. The upside case spends $6,000m on fleet versus $5,350m in base. First-period repurchases are $448m, below base, despite higher operating cash. This is a choice between reinvestment and distributions, not evidence that growth destroys value.

The price requires assumptions this model does not establish. Holding five explicit cash flows fixed, the $1,170.05 quote implies a 64.0% terminal operating margin at 10% WACC, 2.5% growth and 10% terminal ROIC. That unusually demanding conditional result may reflect an incomplete operating or terminal model. It does not prove mispricing.

## Most consequential choice
The most consequential operating choice is the fleet cohort approximation: a legacy depreciation rate, half-year depreciation on new purchases and assumed disposal ages connect investment to fleet capacity and earnings. Exact asset cohorts and utilization are unavailable. Gross fleet starts at a rounded $25bn, so small changes should not be interpreted as precise capacity estimates.

## Best objection
The strongest argument against the low DCF indication is that future fleet productivity, acquisition benefits or sustainable returns could be stronger than this deliberately simplified build captures. Conversely, recent cash conversion may not persist. Terminal value contributes 74.9% of base enterprise value. Changing discount rates and terminal reinvestment can dominate the apparent gap to price.

## Questions to rehearse
1. Why do stronger profits not necessarily increase repurchase capacity?
2. Why does a noncash impairment affect assets and equity without an equal cash outflow?
3. Why use dated cover-page shares for market capitalization instead of weighted-average EPS shares?
4. What happens when refinancing is unavailable? Does the model assume a facility?
5. Why retain stock compensation as an economic cost in DCF?
6. What fraction of value comes after the explicit forecast, and what changes it?
7. Which cost and working-capital assumptions could reverse the conclusion?

## Explain the funding waterfall
Opening cash + CFO + CFI - principal repayment + assumed refinancing. Compare with the cash reserve. Show any additional funding required explicitly. Cap dividends and buybacks to available cash. Sweep residual cash to debt. Link closing cash, debt and equity back to the balance sheet. Interest uses opening debt to avoid circularity.

## Honest closing
Asset-level depreciation and disposal cohorts, owned-fleet utilization by category, rental rate versus mix, maintenance spending and an instrument-level debt maturity schedule would most improve the model. I would improve those inputs before presenting this as an investment recommendation.
